---
title: "How to Address Your Salary Expectations"
date: 2017-05-20
categories: ["Interview Tips", "Job Search", "Career Planning"]
---

The interview process can be daunting, to say the least. From looking professional to being prepared, job seekers are under tremendous pressure. It's easy to forget about one important topic: salary expectations. When an interviewer asks what your salary expectations are for your next role, you should have a clear and coherent answer. There are countless horror stories about job seekers blindly throwing out numbers that make or break their chances of getting hired.

It's a hard topic to discuss if you haven't already thought about your answer and taken the steps to prepare. Take note of the following tips.

**Do the research.** Sit down and find out as much as you can about the going rate for the position you're interviewing for. Salary websites can often be misleading based on location and the scope of responsibility for each job. So ask around, find out what people in your area in a similar role are making, and base your expectations on realistic numbers. You can also reach out to local professional organizations and recruiters to get a better idea of the salary or hourly rates they see for your position.

**Provide a range.** Instead of coming up with a hard number, look at what you're making right now and what other professionals are making in your line of work, and find a range that works for you. Make sure that you will be happy with the low end of whatever range you give. If you say $45K to $50K, you will have to be comfortable with $45K, because that's what you told the employer you'd be OK with.

**Don't play games.** If you're making $45K at your current position and you tell an employer you're looking to make $60K or more, you're treating your career like a game of poker. Asking for an increase over your current salary is completely OK, but be realistic about how much more you're asking for and make sure it's relatively close to what the employer is offering for the position. If a potential employer found out you asked for $15K more than you said you were making, they might consider you untrustworthy and not consider hiring you. Companies can also request W-2s or pay stubs to verify salary, so you don't want to be caught being dishonest.

**Identify why you're valuable.** If you have special skills, certifications, and abilities that the employer would otherwise have to train people in, express the value of already having them. For example, if you're Google-certified in AdWords, those classes can cost up to $500. Go through your resume and gather this information so you can bring it up in salary negotiations.
